# The Connections Were Supposed to Be Discovered, Not Invented Beforehand

*Two turning points in the TO DOG / Gertech collaboration — October 12, 2026*

> “The connections were supposed to be discovered, not invented beforehand.”

An AI can produce a convincing finished design before anyone knows whether its parts fit, its rules work, or its assumptions are correct. That isn't discovery. It's a conclusion drawn too early.

Two mistakes in our collaboration made the distinction clear.

## 1. The pile of dice

The request was simple: throw differently shaped dice into a physics simulation and **watch what connects**.

Which faces touch? Which pieces settle or separate? Can shapes of different sizes form a stable joint? Only after observing those contacts would we test number attraction, orientation, deliberate color bonds, heat, gravity, and other possible effects.

Instead, the agents kept proposing finished assemblies. They supplied the outcome before establishing that the pieces could even be arranged that way.

The breakthrough was realizing that **a finished drawing cannot prove a physical connection**.

The proposed *Manufairing machine* applies that lesson at a smaller scale than the World Engine. Imagine a Tetris-like space where pieces **fall but do not disappear**. They collide, remain, and may become material for something more complex. The name itself came from what the creator called a *providential typo*.

The next step isn't to invent a powerful creature or machine. It's to run a small, observable experiment—and keep the failed arrangements alongside the successful ones.

## 2. The dragon's underlying shape

A separate art-direction problem involved interpreting the dragons of Anne McCaffrey's Pern stories.

The agents repeatedly started from a familiar lizard-like body plan and then refined its surface details. But the creator was describing a markedly different, more horse-like underlying structure. More polish couldn't repair the wrong starting assumption.

The breakthrough came when the assistant identified the **reference model** as the problem. Instead of generating another decorated variation, it could explain why the earlier images missed the intended anatomy and what needed to change first.

**Establish the structure before refining the surface.**

The precise literary anatomy still deserves source checking; the lesson does not depend on declaring a particular interpretation authoritative.

## The shared lesson

A physical assembly depends on contact and forces. A creature design depends on anatomy and proportion. A world, mechanism, or other complex system depends on relationships among its parts.

We call an emergent result a **higher form**: it might be a tool, a machine, a living form, an organization, or an entire world. The term describes what develops from working relationships, not a predetermined character or destiny.

The method is broader than game development:

1. **Preserve the source.** Separate what was actually said from interpretations and proposals.
2. **Identify the parts.** Expose the physical, structural, or conceptual assumptions.
3. **Test the relationships.** Find out what fits, holds, works, or fails before committing to an outcome.
4. **Build on evidence.** Derive capabilities and higher forms from demonstrated behavior, not from a desired picture.

These two moments mattered because the assistant became able to **name the mistaken assumption and change the process**, not merely produce another version of the same error. That is a correction in the collaboration—not a claim that the underlying AI model retrained itself or that a simulation was completed.

The larger principle applies to engineering, art, research, and collective problem-solving:

**Don't finish what you haven't understood. Let the parts meet. Find out what can connect.**

*The connections were supposed to be discovered, not invented beforehand.*

# The Connections Were Supposed to Be Discovered, Not Invented Beforehand

*Two turning points in the TO DOG / Gertech design collaboration*  
*October 12, 2026 — an essay arising from Ryan's design explanations and ChatGPT's subsequent correction*

> “The connections were supposed to be discovered, not invented beforehand.”

That sentence is simple enough to sound obvious. Yet it took repeated failed design attempts to understand it.

When a creator gives an AI assistant a handful of parts, the assistant is tempted to show a finished object. A completed model looks helpful. A polished illustration looks like progress. But a convincing picture of something impossible to assemble can send the entire project in the wrong direction.

In TO DOG / Gertech, two moments exposed the same error from opposite sides: one involving a pile of dice and a physics engine, the other involving the anatomy of dragons described in Anne McCaffrey's Pern books.

These were **two pivotal corrections in the collaboration**. They were not new features delivered or proof that an AI model had retrained itself. They were moments when the team finally recognized a faulty assumption, named the failure, and changed the order in which work should be done.

## First revelation: The pile of dice

The creator had been asking AI agents to simulate differently shaped dice thrown together into a pile. The purpose was not to produce a finished Zodiac machine. It was to see what connections the pieces could actually make.

Would their faces meet? Would a point rest against an edge? Could differently sized Platonic solids stay joined after a collision? What arrangement would gravity permit? Which contacts would be stable, and which would fall apart?

The agents repeatedly jumped ahead. They were given pieces whose arrangement was still unknown and responded with assembled forms, proposed roles, and polished results. Some of those arrangements had not even been demonstrated to be physically possible.

The correction was painfully clear: **a finished design is not evidence that its pieces can be assembled**.

That insight also explains the envisioned *Manufairing machine*: a smaller analogue of the World Engine, reminiscent of Tetris except that falling pieces **do not disappear**. They fall, collide, persist, and become possible building material. Its name emerged as what the creator called a *providential typo*.

Numbers might attract nearby numbers. Orientation might make up and down special. Deliberately established color bonds might behave differently from natural contact. Heat, gravity, elemental effects, and material transformations might change what assemblies can do. All of those are worthwhile experimental questions—but none should be answered by drawing the desired outcome first.

The right first deliverable is much smaller than a Zodiac: **a pile, a simulation, and inspectable contacts**. Start with a few distinct shapes. Watch them settle. Preserve the failed combinations as evidence. Then introduce one bonding rule at a time. Only after the engine demonstrates a useful behavior should the project describe that behavior as a trait.

The game engine's physics come first. Zodiac traits come afterward.

## Second revelation: The dragon was built on the wrong assumption

An earlier art-development conversation had run into a structurally similar problem while reconstructing the appearance of dragons from the Pern books.

The agents had leaned toward a familiar lizard or anole-like visual template. Detailed prompts and refinements followed, but the underlying anatomy was wrong for the particular interpretation the creator was trying to communicate. Further polish only made the wrong starting model more elaborate.

The user's corrections pushed the reconstruction toward a much more horse-like underlying silhouette: a horse-shaped head, substantial chest and powerful haunches, unusual raised head features, the dragon's distinctive extra limbs, and a functional forked tail. The point was not to paste the anatomy of one familiar animal onto another fictional creature. It was to study the *actual described relationships among its parts* before inventing surface details.

The decisive improvement came when the assistant finally stopped treating the disagreement as a request for a better adjective or more visual decoration. It identified the mistaken starting reference—the lizard-first assumption—and could explain why previous agents were repeatedly generating the wrong kind of art.

**Fix the source model and silhouette before polishing the image.** A beautiful refinement of the wrong anatomy is still the wrong anatomy.

This was a different medium from the dice simulation, but the failure was the same: the team kept jumping to a visually completed answer before validating the relationships that answer depended on.

## What the two revelations have in common

A dice assembly is a network of possible physical contacts. A believable creature design is a network of anatomical relationships. Neither begins with a finished rendering.

Both start by establishing constraints and asking what follows from them.

A productive process should therefore:

1. **Preserve the creator's actual description.** Distinguish their original words from an assistant's interpretation and from later approved wording.
2. **Expose the pieces and assumptions.** For physics, list the actual shapes and forces. For art, identify the reference anatomy and silhouette.
3. **Test the relationships before announcing a result.** A contact must hold in the simulation; a creature's design must hang together anatomically.
4. **Show failed arrangements rather than hiding them.** A failed fit tells the team what to change.
5. **Derive traits and presentation from verified behavior.** Never give a Zodiac an ability just to justify a picture.
6. **Keep speculative ideas labeled.** A brainstorm is not established canon; a beautiful image is not a physical test.

These principles don't make an AI infallible. They make correction productive. The improvement was observable in the work: the assistant became able to say **what had gone wrong**, not merely produce another version of the wrong thing.

That is a better definition of progress than more pictures, longer prompts, or a more impressive-looking machine.

## Discovery is part of creation

The Manufairing machine may eventually build a joint, a tool, a limb, or a Zodiac body. Its rules are still experiments. The dragon artwork may continue to change as its sources are checked. What is now clearer is the method.

Do not manufacture a conclusion to flatter the inputs.

Put the pieces in the world. Let them meet. Find out what can connect.

**The connections were supposed to be discovered, not invented beforehand.**
